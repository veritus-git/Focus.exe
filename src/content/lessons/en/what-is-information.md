# What is Information?

Before we dive into processors, artificial intelligence, or cryptography — we must travel back thousands, even billions of years. Long before electricity, integrated circuits, and screens were ever invented, information was already ruling our world.

To truly understand what a "bit" is, you first need to feel the massive problem that nature and humanity have been battling since the very dawn of existence. What exactly is information?

---PAGE---

## The Universe Trends Toward Death (The Principle of Entropy)

Let's start with the hardest physics. The Second Law of Thermodynamics tells us a rather depressing truth: the universe naturally tends toward chaos, decay, and maximum disorder. Physicists call this phenomenon **entropy**.

Leave a cup of hot tea on a table, and it will cool down to match its surroundings. Build a magnificent sandcastle, and the wind and waves will flatten it. Leave an iron sword out in the rain, and rust will consume it. Things do not spontaneously become more organized. They always march toward destruction.

What is the only force in the universe capable of standing up to entropy? What can take chaos and turn it into order?

**It is information.**

Information is structure. It is a precise recipe for how to arrange atoms, how to arrange sounds, how to arrange words. Information is everything that defies blind chaos.

---PAGE---

## The Source Code of Life (Your First Operating System)

Nature understood the power of information billions of years ago. When the very first life forms appeared on Earth, biology had to solve a colossal problem: how do you write down the building instructions for a living organism and pass it on before that organism dies?

This is how **DNA** was created. It is nothing more than a biological flash drive, a giant tape holding source code.

However, notice something fascinating: nature didn't use a binary system (consisting of two states). It built this code using four chemical molecules: Adenine (A), Cytosine (C), Guanine (G), and Thymine (T). Instead of zeros and ones, life is a string of letters like: `A-G-G-T-C-A-T...`

Every cell in your body contains billions of these "letters". When a copying error occurs in this code (a letter gets swapped) — we call it a mutation. This was our planet's first form of processing and storing data. Whoever could better process information about their environment, survived evolution.

## The Human Superpower and External Hard Drives

Why did we, humans (Homo sapiens), dominate this planet? We are not the fastest, we don't have the sharpest teeth, we can't fly, and we lack thick armor.

We won because we mastered **transmitting information between minds on an unprecedented scale**.

In the beginning, it was just language. We could warn our tribe that "there are lions across the river". The problem with speech, however, was that words vanished. They were like RAM memory — they disappeared the moment the oldest wise man in the village died, or when a story was twisted by successive generations (like in a game of telephone).

Then came the greatest invention before the computer era. We realized we could externalize information, throwing it outside our own brains.
The Sumerians began pressing symbols into clay tablets to count bags of grain. Those clay tablets were humanity's very first **hard drives**. We conquered time. The knowledge of an architect, a medic, or a king could survive hundreds of years after their death, teaching subsequent generations.

## The Nightmare of the Analog Era (The Tower of Babel Problem)

For thousands of years, we developed ways of recording, but we backed ourselves into a massive technological corner. Different types of information required completely different, incompatible physical mediums.

- Want to record thoughts and stories? You use ink and paper.
- Want to record what a ruler looked like? You hire a painter who uses paints and canvas.
- Want to record music from a concert? You carve mechanical grooves into a vinyl record that physically mimic the sound wave.
- Want to record motion? You expose photographic film, frame by frame.

It was a nightmare. You couldn't send an image through a telegraph (which only sent taps of Morse code). You couldn't print sound in a book. Every type of information was locked inside its own physical realm. They were like tribes speaking different languages, entirely unable to communicate with each other.

We needed one, absolutely universal language.

---PAGE---

## The Father of the Digital World and Reducing Uncertainty

In 1948, one man solved this problem. His name was Claude Shannon. While working on encryption and telephony systems, he published a groundbreaking scientific paper that changed humanity forever.

Shannon asked a brilliantly simple question: "How can we measure the amount of information, regardless of whether it's text, sound, or an image?"

He noticed that acquiring new knowledge is actually just the **reduction of uncertainty**.
Imagine playing "20 Questions". You have to guess what animal I'm thinking of. With every "Yes/No" question you ask (Is it a mammal? Can it fly? Is it a predator?), you eliminate half of the incorrect possibilities. Your uncertainty decreases.

Shannon concluded that if you ask enough "YES" or "NO" questions, you can describe absolutely everything in the universe.

This single division, this one "Yes or No", "On or Off" answer — is a **bit** (short for *binary digit*). The smallest possible, indivisible atom in the digital world.

---PAGE---

## The Great Unification: Everything Becomes Digital

Shannon's discovery sparked a revolution. Suddenly, we understood that canvases, video tapes, and vinyl records were no longer mandatory. Every piece of information could be reduced to a giant series of "Yes or No" questions — a massive list of zeros and ones.

- **A photo?** We slice it into millions of microscopic dots (pixels). We describe each dot using numbers (how much red, green, and blue it contains).
- **A song?** Instead of carving grooves in vinyl, we simply measure the air pressure 44,100 times every single second. Each measurement is recorded as a number.
- **Text?** We throw away the ink. We assign a predetermined value to each letter. The letter 'A' is the number 65. The letter 'B' is 66.
- **A 3D Game Environment?** Your position (X,Y,Z), the angle of the sun, the texture of the hero's armor — it's all just math updated 60 times a second.

Suddenly, texts, songs, movies, and complex software started flowing through the exact same, universal channel. The only difference between them was how many of these "bits" they carried.

## Enter the Machines (Why Electricity?)

Once we reduced everything to numbers (zeros and ones), we needed machines that could store and calculate them in the blink of an eye.

But how do you force physics to calculate? We looked for a phenomenon that was incredibly fast. There was only one answer: **electricity**.

And how do you control electricity? With switches. A switch is the perfect representation of a bit because it has exactly two states:
1. **Current flows (ON)** — which means a one.
2. **Current does not flow (OFF)** — which means a zero.

The brutal laws of physics forced us to use the binary (base-2) system. Imagine trying to use a decimal system and transmitting 10 different voltage values (e.g., 1 Volt is the digit 1, 9 Volts is the digit 9).
Cables heat up, thunderstorms cause surges, and other machines operating nearby emit magnetic fields. There is "noise" everywhere. If a computer sends a "3 Volts" signal, but due to interference it arrives as "3.5 Volts", the machine gets confused. It starts mixing up digits.

That is why we use zeros and ones. The boundary is simple. "Is the voltage above a certain threshold?" -> That means YES (1). "Is the voltage close to zero?" -> That means NO (0). The binary system is the computer's way of surviving in a chaotic, noise-filled physical world.

> [!KEY] Information is a rebellion against entropy. It is organized structure and the reduction of our uncertainty about the world. The entire modern digital realm — from 4K movies, to SMS messages, to AI language models — is a unified system of asking billions of "Yes or No" questions, physically represented by switches letting electrical current flow or blocking it.
