# What is Information?

> "The universe is a clock winding down, and we are the gears trying to stop it."

Before we dive into processors, artificial intelligence, or cryptography — we must travel back billions of years. Long before electricity and screens were ever invented, information was already ruling our world.

But what exactly is it?

---PAGE---

## The Universe Hates Order

Physics tells us a brutal truth: **everything naturally tends toward chaos and decay**. Things do not spontaneously get better, they simply fade away.

| Real-Life Example | The Effect of Entropy |
| :--- | :--- |
| **A sandcastle** | Waves wash it away, turning it into a flat beach. |
| **Hot tea** | Cools down, giving its heat back to the environment. |
| **An iron bridge** | Slowly eaten alive by rust over the years. |

Scientists call this **entropy**. It is blind, destructive chaos, the ultimate destiny of all matter.

But what is the only force in the universe capable of standing up to entropy?

**Information.**

Information is a rebellion against chaos. It is structure. It is a precise recipe for how to arrange atoms, how to arrange sounds, how to arrange words.

> 🧬 **The Code of Life**
> Nature figured this out billions of years ago. Your DNA code isn't just some "biology" — it is literally 3 gigabytes of pure, hard information. It is a survival manual written using four molecules (A, C, G, T), telling your body how not to disintegrate. Whoever can store information, lives.

## Humanity's External Hard Drives

Why did we, humans, dominate this planet? We are not the fastest, we don't have the sharpest teeth, nor do we have thick armor.

We won because we mastered **transmitting information between minds**.

In the beginning, it was just speech. We could warn someone about a predator. But speech was like volatile RAM memory — it vanished the moment the oldest person in the village died. So we did something brilliant. We started "throwing" information outside our brains.

> 🗿 Cave paintings and clay tablets were humanity's very first **hard drives**. We conquered time. The knowledge of one person could survive hundreds of years and teach someone born much later.

## The Curse of the Analog Era

However, we had a massive technological problem. Different types of information required completely different, **incompatible physical mediums**.

Imagine a world where every single sense requires a different technology:

| Type of Information | Analog Medium (The Past) | The Biggest Problem |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **History / Text** | Ink and paper | Flammable, fragile, hard to copy |
| **Images / Faces** | Canvas and paint | Extremely slow to record, 100% subjective |
| **Sound / Music** | Grooves in a vinyl record | Mechanically worn out with every single playback |

It was a nightmare. You couldn't send an image through a telegraph. You couldn't print sound in a book. Information formats were like warring tribes speaking different languages, entirely unable to communicate.

We needed **one, absolutely universal language**, capable of connecting all human senses.

---PAGE---

## Reducing Uncertainty (The 20 Questions Game)

This universal language is based on one brilliant observation by engineers: acquiring information is simply the **reduction of uncertainty**.

Imagine we are playing "20 Questions". You have to guess what animal I'm thinking of. With every "Yes/No" question you ask (Is it a mammal? Does it fly? Is it a predator?), you eliminate half of the wrong possibilities. Your uncertainty drops drastically.
If you ask enough "Yes or No" questions, you can describe absolutely everything in the universe.

> 💡 **A bit is nothing more than the answer to a single question.** 
> This single division, this single "Yes or No", "On or Off" — is exactly what a **bit** is. The smallest, indivisible atom of the digital world.

---PAGE---

## The Digital Illusion (Everything is Numbers)

Thanks to bits, we realized we don't need canvases, paper, or vinyl at all. Every piece of information can be reduced to a massive list of "Yes or No" questions — meaning zeros and ones.

Inside a computer, there is no such thing as an "image file" or "music". The computer is blind and deaf. It converts human experiences into **pure mathematics**:

> 🖼️ **An Image (Photos)**
> We slice space into millions of microscopic squares (pixels). We describe each pixel using just 3 numbers (values for Red, Green, and Blue).

> 🎵 **Sound (Music)**
> We measure air pressure (a sound wave) exactly 44,100 times every single second. We record each measurement as a specific number.

> 📝 **Text (This Article)**
> We assign a predetermined number to each letter (the ASCII standard). E.g., the letter 'A' is 65, 'B' is 66, and Space is 32.

> 🎮 **3D Reality (Video Games)**
> Your position (X, Y, Z), your character's heartbeat, and the wind momentum are just giant arrays of numbers updated 60 times a second by the graphics card.

Everything started flowing through the exact same, universal channel of electricity.

## Why Zeros and Ones?

Many people think zeros and ones are some perfect mathematical invention. The truth is much more brutal. We use them because of the **laws of physics**.

Since we reduced everything to numbers, we had to build machines to calculate them. We used electricity. And the easiest way to control electricity is with switches.

Imagine trying to build a computer that uses digits from 0 to 9. We would have to send 10 different voltage levels (e.g., 1 Volt is digit 1, 9 Volts is digit 9).

But we live in a brutal physical world:
- Cables heat up.
- Thunderstorms cause power surges.
- There is electromagnetic interference (noise) all around us.

If a cable sends a "3 Volts" signal, and due to interference it arrives as "3.5 Volts", the computer gets confused and starts mixing up digits.

That is why we use zeros and ones. The boundary is bulletproof. **"CURRENT IS FLOWING" is a one. "NO CURRENT" is a zero.** Even if the signal is slightly noisy, the computer immediately knows what it's dealing with. Physical reliability forced us to use the binary system.

## How Does the Computer Know What It's Looking At?

There is one more massive problem. If a holiday photo and a text document are both just strings of ones and zeros underneath... how does the computer know if the sequence `01000001` means the letter "A", or a piece of a green pixel from a game?

The answer is **context**.

By themselves, bits are just dead electricity. They mean absolutely nothing. It is the software that gives them meaning.

If you open this string of bits in Notepad, the program will say: *"Ah, I'm loading a text file, so this must be a letter of the alphabet"*. If you open the exact same bits in Photoshop, the program will treat them as information about light intensity.

> **Bits are just clay. Software dictates its shape.**
